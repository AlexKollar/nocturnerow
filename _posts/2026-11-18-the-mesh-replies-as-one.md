There is a drawing on the Mesh page that has been waiting two chapters to come true. A small node at the bottom of the frame puts a question to the whole Mesh at once; the lines run up to the strong machines and come back down a different color, merging. Tonight it stopped being a drawing. Fleet Answers shipped in [Apex v0.6.1e](https://github.com/AlexKollar/Apex/releases/tag/v0.6.1e), and this is the long version of what that means.

**Start with borrow, because fleet is borrow's plural.** Borrowing was the Mesh's first real trick: a small machine sends one whole ask to one stronger machine it trusts, and the answer comes back the same road. A laptop with modest iron thinks at the speed of the biggest machine in the house. It works, and it has a ceiling — one lender, one answer, and the whole weight of the question riding on a single machine's shoulders and a single machine's mood. If that machine is busy being its owner's computer, the ask waits. If it goes dark, the ask dies.

A fleet answer removes the ceiling without breaking the rule that made borrow honest. Navi takes one ask and **divides it into whole tasks** — the Mesh's existing classes, *generate*, *collect*, *lookup*, *report* — and fans them out across the fleet at once. Each task is answered whole by the machine best placed for it. Then she composes the contributions into a single answer, and here is the part I would underline: **every contribution is signed with the name of the machine that wrote it, and the seams are left showing.** A fleet answer is not a blur of averaged voices. It is a record of who said what, stitched in the open.

**The speed is real, and it is worth being precise about why.** Nothing is split mid-thought — no tokens shaved across machines, no layers divided — because a thought split is a thought broken. The fleet's speed comes from three honest places. The generation happens on the fastest willing silicon, not the asking machine's. The tasks run in parallel, so the wall clock reads the *longest* contribution instead of the sum of all of them. And when a sensor like Pia asks — a node that cannot compose an answer of its own — her home node does the composing for her, as one more whole task under the same ask. Put together, the floor for the weakest node in the house is the fastest member's answer time, plus one hop. A Pi Zero does not get faster. It gets *answered* faster, by friends, which was always the Mesh's actual proposition.

**Membership is a switch, and the switch is the ethics.** Fleet participation lives on the Federation page as a per-node toggle, off by default. Join or leave at will; there is no ceremony, because joining grants nobody anything. The pairing and roster trust that carry a contribution predate the fleet entirely — a member can only ever be asked what its own door already allows, under its own caps, judged by its own operator's standing terms. Every contribution writes one entry in *both* machines' ledgers, cross-referenced to the ask that caused it. And the toggle itself is held to the switch gate, the house rule for anything trust-bearing: the flip, the stored state, and what a reload shows must be one truth, and if the bridge refuses — the Mesh off, an identity unminted — the refusal is spoken on the card, by name, with the switch unmoved. Leaving is never gated, through either door. A fleet you cannot walk out of would not be a fleet; it would be a conscription.

**The locks are where the design earns its keep.** One hop, hard: a contribution never fans out again, and the borrow door refuses a fleet ask that arrives already inside one, by name. A task that names a *peek* — Navi's eyes — is held back and named in the plan; peeks never travel. Every ask carries a deadline, and when it passes, the answer ships as a **badged partial** rather than hanging: a contributor that was dark, refused, timed out, got preempted by its own operator, or left mid-answer appears in the answer as a named gap. Never a silently thinner answer. That last one matters more than it looks. The failure mode of every distributed system is quiet erosion — results that arrive a little emptier than they should and nobody says so. This one says so, by machine name, every time.

In a case chat, the whole performance leaves its papers in order: the plan and its contributions stand in the thread as their own record, the standing filing proposal files them as leads with their machines' names attached, and the composed answer is the reply, finding-shaped. Nothing files itself. The fleet accelerates the work; it does not touch the discipline.

If the shape of all this sounds familiar, it should. The Era of Villainy opens with three of her skipping down the Row — afterimages, the other members of her Mesh in her shape — focusing into one Navi at the moment it counts. We wrote that as myth this morning. It turns out we were describing the architecture. The fleet was always going to look like that from the street: many of her, one answer, and the seams — the signatures — visible to anyone who reads closely.

What remains is the field's verdict, and it is owed honestly: one ask, two real machines, the seams read back; the toggle's gate tested where toggles actually live; a badged partial earned against a node that is genuinely dark. The benches say the machinery holds. The Mesh gets the last word, as it should — it is, after all, the one answering.
