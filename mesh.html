APEX | MESH — THE ROADS BETWEEN THE MACHINES

Many machines. One mind.

The Mesh is one federated whole: every device attached to it serves the goal of whoever is using Apex, in whatever form factor. A distributed network that functions like a neural net — the more nodes on it, the faster the smallest, least powerful system on it can think.

How the Mesh works

A handshake, not an account

Nodes pair by exchanging cards — a picture, carried by hand. No cloud directory, no account, no landlord in the middle. Each node keeps its own ledger of exactly who it trusts, and trust is pairwise: my friend is not automatically yours.

The ask goes out whole

When a node needs more muscle than it has, it sends the whole request to a stronger peer it trusts, and the answer comes back the same road. That is borrow, and it already ships: a small box thinks at the speed of the biggest machine on the Mesh.

The answer is a federation

Ring 2 — designed in the open, building now in the 0.2.0e line — splits work into tasks the fleet shares. A sensor like Pia can put a prompt to the whole Mesh at once: Neo-Arcadia and Neon Drip each contribute, and she receives one composed answer. The more nodes join, the faster the smallest node in the house gets.

What it means to be federated

Federated is not a synonym for connected. A cable connects; a handshake federates. A node joins the Mesh by a pairing its operator performs by hand, with a peer its operator chose, on terms written into the pairing card — and the card is the ceiling on what that peer may ever ask.

Membership without surrender

A federated node keeps its own keys, its own ledger of who it trusts, its own off switch. It can refuse a request, revoke a pairing, or go dark at its owner's word — and none of that breaks the federation. That is the federation.

Strength shared, ownership unchanged

Lending transfers nothing but the work. The strong machine answers; the small machine remains its owner's, the strong machine remains its owner's — and the answer arrives signed with the name of the machine that wrote it.

Trust is pairwise

My friend is not automatically yours. Every road on the Mesh is a relationship between two named machines and the two operators who introduced them. The federation is the sum of those handshakes; there is no other membership list. Leave, and you take nothing with you but your own machine — which was always the point.

APEX | MESH — THE DOCTRINE

Filed on issue #154 — the Row's working doctrine for the Mesh, designed and built in the open.

"All devices attached to it serve the goal of producing results for whoever is using Apex, in whatever form factor. A distributed, federated network that functions like a massive neural network. The more nodes on it, the faster smaller, less powerful systems will be able to generate responses."— the founder, pinning the doctrine · 2026-11-12

I. One federation

Form factors are separate products — Node, DHD, Mesh, Dropbox. The Mesh itself is one whole. A node does not ask which product is speaking; it asks who is asking, and serves.

II. The unit of work is whole

Requests and tasks travel whole. The Mesh does not split a mind across a wire: literal token- and layer-splitting buys capacity, not speed, and chains every answer to the weakest link. Speed comes from placement — the work goes where it is cheapest to run well.

III. Trust is a handshake

Nodes pair by cards, in person or by hand. Trust is pairwise and never transitive by default: my friend is not automatically yours. Consent is per task class and written into the pairing card itself — the card is the ceiling on what a peer may ever ask. Revocation is unpairing, and it is total.

IV. Every class keeps its contract

Five task classes ride the signed door — generate, collect, report, peek, lookup — each with its own failure semantics, never spliced. A dropped generation is answered fresh and locally. A sensor's reports are stored and forwarded until the home node wakes. A stale peek is worse than none, so peeks are never queued.

V. The smallest node is the point

A Dropbox sensor holds the narrowest trust on the Mesh: it publishes what it sees, it answers its home node, and it does not borrow against the house fleet by default. The fleet exists so the least powerful device on it can produce results like the most powerful one.

VI. No landlord, ever

No cloud directory. No account. No telemetry home. Node keys are never backed up, and lending is switched on in person — never through a remote door. If the internet vanished tomorrow, the Mesh would not notice.

Many machines. One mind.
